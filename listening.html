01

Baroque · c. 1720

J. S. Bach — Partita No. 2 in D minor, BWV 1004

Nathan Milstein, violin

The monumental Chaconne at the end of this partita is one of the great achievements of solo instrumental music. Milstein's interpretation is admired for its structural clarity, singing line, and ability to sustain a vast span without sacrificing intimacy. It is an especially rewarding introduction to the idea that a single violin can imply a whole musical world.

02

Baroque · 1720s

Vivaldi — The Four Seasons

Julia Fischer, violin · Academy of St Martin in the Fields

Vivaldi's concertos turn seasonal scenes into vivid musical gestures: icy tremolos, birdsong-like figures, storms, and pastoral dances. Fischer's performance offers clarity and animation while keeping the concertos' theatrical contrasts in view. It is a useful reminder that the Baroque concerto can be both formally inventive and immediately descriptive.

03

Classical · 1775

Mozart — Violin Concerto No. 4 in D major, K. 218

Arthur Grumiaux, violin

Grumiaux's Mozart is a model of poise, elegance, and natural phrasing. Rather than exaggerating the concerto's virtuosity, he allows its conversational wit and graceful proportions to come forward. This is a particularly fine recording for hearing how much expression can be achieved through apparent simplicity.

04

Romantic · 1844

Mendelssohn — Violin Concerto in E minor, Op. 64

Jascha Heifetz, violin

Heifetz brings tensile energy, brilliant articulation, and an almost startling command of the concerto's technical writing. Mendelssohn's work is one of the most influential Romantic concertos, notable for beginning with the soloist rather than a long orchestral exposition and for linking its movements unusually closely.

Romantic · 1866

Bruch — Violin Concerto No. 1 in G minor, Op. 26

David Oistrakh, violin

Oistrakh's broad, burnished sound suits Bruch's expansive melodies particularly well. The concerto's central Adagio is among the most beloved lyrical episodes in the repertoire, and Oistrakh gives it warmth without sentimentality. This recording also connects a major Romantic concerto with one of the twentieth century's defining violinists.

Romantic · 1878

Brahms — Violin Concerto in D major, Op. 77

Isaac Stern, violin · Eugene Ormandy, Philadelphia Orchestra

Stern's performance emphasizes the concerto's breadth and symphonic character. Brahms's writing asks the soloist to be both a virtuoso and a chamber musician, listening closely to the orchestra while sustaining a long-range musical argument. The Adagio's oboe solo and the finale's Hungarian-inflected rhythms are especially memorable.

Romantic · 1878

Tchaikovsky — Violin Concerto in D major, Op. 35

Itzhak Perlman, violin · Herbert von Karajan, Berlin Philharmonic

Perlman's warmth and vocal phrasing bring out the concerto's emotional directness, while Karajan supplies a richly colored orchestral frame. The performance captures both the work's sweeping lyricism and its high-spirited finale. It is a compelling example of the concerto as a meeting of solo personality and orchestral scale.

08

Romantic · 1878

Sarasate — Zigeunerweisen, Op. 20

Anne-Sophie Mutter, violin

A brilliant showpiece that moves from a dramatic introduction through expressive song to a dazzling finale. Mutter combines technical sparkle with a strong sense of character, showing how virtuoso repertoire can be theatrical without becoming superficial. The work's stylized Roma-inspired idiom should be heard in its nineteenth-century context rather than mistaken for documentary folk music.

Late Romantic · 1904–05

Sibelius — Violin Concerto in D minor, Op. 47

Hilary Hahn, violin · Esa-Pekka Salonen, Swedish Radio Symphony Orchestra

Hahn's focused sound and exacting control illuminate the concerto's austere atmosphere and formidable architecture. The opening solo line seems to emerge from a cold, spacious landscape; the finale's rhythmic drive feels almost elemental. This performance is a strong entry point to Sibelius's distinctive musical world.

10

20th century · 1947–48

Shostakovich — Violin Concerto No. 1 in A minor, Op. 77

David Oistrakh, violin · Dimitri Mitropoulos, New York Philharmonic

Oistrakh was the concerto's dedicatee and first performer, and his interpretation remains central to the work's identity. The Nocturne's inward intensity, the Scherzo's biting energy, and the Passacaglia's monumental gravity all demand a soloist who can combine power with restraint. The work's delayed premiere also places it in the cultural history of the late Stalin years.

11

20th century · 1938

Bartók — Violin Concerto No. 2, Sz. 112

Midori, violin

Bartók's concerto is a meeting of expansive lyricism, folk-derived rhythmic language, and modernist construction. Midori's concentrated approach makes the work's shifting characters especially clear. The variation-form slow movement is a highlight, showing how a simple theme can generate a remarkable range of color and emotion.

12

20th century · 1935

Berg — Violin Concerto “To the Memory of an Angel”

Isabelle Faust, violin

Faust brings clarity and expressive concentration to a work that can seem forbidding at first. Berg's twelve-tone language is interwoven with tonal and historical references, and the concerto's final chorale creates a moving sense of transformation. This is an essential recording for listeners curious about how modernism can communicate profound emotion.

20th century · 1967

Shostakovich — Violin Concerto No. 2 in C-sharp minor, Op. 129

David Oistrakh, violin · Kirill Kondrashin, Moscow Philharmonic

Oistrakh premiered this concerto, written for his sixtieth birthday. Its darker, more compressed language rewards close listening: the first movement's brooding restraint gives way to an uneasy, sardonic middle movement and a finale of hard-edged virtuosity. The recording is a direct link to the composer-performer relationship at the heart of the work.

14

20th century · 1917

Prokofiev — Violin Concerto No. 1 in D major, Op. 19

Augustin Hadelich, violin

Prokofiev's First Concerto is dreamlike, mercurial, and full of unexpected shifts. Its opening movement begins with a lyrical melody that seems to float above the orchestra, while the finale ends with an unusually delicate, almost vanishing gesture. Hadelich's tonal refinement and control make the concerto's contrasts especially vivid.

15

Modern recording · Baroque repertoire

J. S. Bach — Chaconne from Partita No. 2 in D minor

Hilary Hahn, violin

Hahn's interpretation offers a compelling contrast to Milstein's: clear, disciplined, and deeply attentive to the music's architecture. The Chaconne's variations unfold over a recurring harmonic pattern, moving through grief, intensity, and a radiant major-key section before returning to D minor. Hearing two different readings of the same work is one of the best ways to understand interpretation.

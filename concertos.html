Chapter III · The repertoire

The violin
concerto

A conversation between one instrument and an orchestra—sometimes graceful, sometimes intimate, sometimes a test of endurance and fire.

A listening route

From first encounter
to the summit

The concerto is an ideal way to discover the violin's expressive range. The soloist stands in dialogue with the orchestra, moving between collaboration and contrast. The works below are arranged in broad tiers according to a combination of listening accessibility, familiarity, and the demands often placed on the soloist.

Important: this is not an objective difficulty ranking. A work that sounds approachable may be technically formidable, and a listener's experience depends on taste, familiarity, and performance. The tiers are intended as a welcoming route into the repertoire.

01

First encounters

Melody, clarity, and immediate appeal

These works offer memorable themes and clear musical narratives, making them rewarding entry points.

1866RomanticApproachable entry point

Max Bruch · Violin Concerto No. 1 in G minor, Op. 26

Bruch's First Concerto is one of the most beloved works in the repertoire. Its opening is more like a free-form prelude than a conventional first movement, moving between brooding orchestral gestures and an expressive solo line. The central Adagio is especially famous for its long, heartfelt melody, while the finale brings rhythmic drive and a triumphant finish. The work's Romantic warmth makes it immediately communicative, even though its technical demands are substantial.

Listen for: the transition from the lyrical Adagio into the energetic finale. It is a concerto built around memorable melodic expression rather than constant display.

Notable interpreters: Jascha Heifetz, David Oistrakh, Itzhak Perlman, and Gil Shaham.

1775ClassicalApproachable entry point

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart · Violin Concerto No. 4 in D major, K. 218

Mozart's Fourth Concerto is elegant, theatrical, and full of conversational detail. Written when he was still in his teens, it reflects the Classical ideal of balance: the soloist is brilliant without overpowering the orchestra. The finale is especially distinctive, with a dance-like character and unexpected changes of mood. Its apparent lightness should not be mistaken for simplicity; the player must make every phrase speak naturally and maintain a graceful sense of proportion.

Listen for: the soloist's playful exchanges with the orchestra and the refined character of the slow movement.

Notable interpreters: Arthur Grumiaux, Anne-Sophie Mutter, and Rachel Podger.

02

Expanding horizons

Long lines and larger emotional worlds

These concertos ask for a more sustained sense of structure, color, and dramatic pacing.

1878RomanticIntermediate listening

Johannes Brahms · Violin Concerto in D major, Op. 77

Brahms's concerto is symphonic in scale. The orchestra is not simply an accompaniment; it is a full dramatic partner, and the soloist must project a coherent line through dense textures. The first movement's expansive architecture, the famous oboe melody that opens the Adagio, and the finale's Hungarian-inspired rhythmic character make the work a journey through contrasting musical worlds. Brahms wrote it for Joseph Joachim, whose advice helped shape the solo part.

Listen for: the oboe's opening statement in the Adagio, and the way the finale's dance rhythms transform the concerto's atmosphere.

Notable interpreters: David Oistrakh, Jascha Heifetz, Isaac Stern, and Julia Fischer.

1878RomanticIntermediate listening

Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky · Violin Concerto in D major, Op. 35

Written during a period of personal upheaval, Tchaikovsky's concerto turns emotional intensity into brilliant orchestral and solo writing. Its opening movement moves from a poised introduction into a sweeping, lyrical theme; the Canzonetta offers a moment of inward reflection; and the finale bursts into folk-like energy. The concerto was initially regarded by some critics as unplayable and excessively difficult, but it became one of the central Romantic concertos.

Listen for: the contrast between the Canzonetta's intimacy and the finale's rhythmic exuberance.

Notable interpreters: Jascha Heifetz, David Oistrakh, Itzhak Perlman, and Augustin Hadelich.

1904–05Late Romantic / ModernIntermediate–advanced

Jean Sibelius · Violin Concerto in D minor, Op. 47

Sibelius's concerto has a distinctive atmosphere: cool, spacious, and intensely concentrated. The violin seems to emerge from a northern landscape, at first alone against a shimmering orchestral background. The first movement combines an expansive solo line with tightly controlled development; the slow movement is deeply lyrical; and the finale, sometimes described as a “polonaise for polar bears,” has a muscular, dance-like drive. The work demands both virtuosity and an unusual sense of atmosphere.

Listen for: the opening solo entrance, the broad melodic arc of the Adagio, and the finale's stamping rhythmic character.

Notable interpreters: Jascha Heifetz, Leonid Kogan, Hilary Hahn, and Augustin Hadelich.

03

Advanced listening

Modernism, tension, and psychological depth

These works often demand unusual stamina, rhythmic precision, and the ability to sustain a complex dramatic argument.

1947–48 · premiered 195520th centuryAdvanced

Dmitri Shostakovich · Violin Concerto No. 1 in A minor, Op. 77

Written for David Oistrakh in 1947–48, the concerto was withheld from performance during the late Stalin period and premiered in October 1955, after Stalin's death. Its four movements form an unusually searching journey: the opening Nocturne is dark and inward; the Scherzo is biting and restless; the Passacaglia builds a grave, monumental emotional center; and the Burlesque finale unleashes fierce energy. Oistrakh's association with the work is fundamental, and he helped the composer refine aspects of the solo writing.

Listen for: the long Passacaglia and the cadenza that grows out of it. The concerto's virtuosity is inseparable from its psychological and historical weight.

Notable interpreters: David Oistrakh (the dedicatee and first performer), Maxim Vengerov, and Lisa Batiashvili.

193520th centuryAdvanced

Alban Berg · Violin Concerto “To the Memory of an Angel”

Berg's concerto is both a modernist landmark and an unusually moving memorial. Dedicated to Manon Gropius, who died at eighteen, it traces a journey from a gentle portrait of youth toward a transformed, elegiac conclusion. Berg combines twelve-tone technique with tonal references, including a Bach chorale and a Carinthian folk song. The result is a work in which modern musical language and emotional directness are closely intertwined.

Listen for: the sudden arrival of the Bach chorale in the final section, which changes the emotional perspective of the work.

Notable interpreters: Louis Krasner (the dedicatee and first performer), Anne-Sophie Mutter, and Isabelle Faust.

04

Demanding landmarks

Virtuosity at the edge

These concertos combine formidable technical writing with distinctive musical and dramatic demands.

196720th centuryVery demanding

Dmitri Shostakovich · Violin Concerto No. 2 in C-sharp minor, Op. 129

Composed in 1967 for David Oistrakh's sixtieth birthday, Shostakovich's Second Concerto is a more concentrated and enigmatic work than its predecessor. Its three movements move from a brooding Moderato to a sardonic, rhythmically alert Adagio and a finale that is both athletic and strangely unsettled. The writing is technically demanding, but its real challenge is tonal and interpretive: the soloist must navigate a world of irony, restraint, and sudden intensity without losing the music's underlying architecture.

Listen for: the uneasy atmosphere of the opening and the strange, almost grotesque energy of the finale.

Notable interpreters: David Oistrakh (first performer), Gidon Kremer, and Isabelle Faust.

193820th centuryVery demanding

Béla Bartók · Violin Concerto No. 2, Sz. 112

Bartók's Second Concerto combines sweeping lyricism with folk-inspired rhythms and a sophisticated variation structure. The first movement unfolds with a broad, searching theme; the slow movement presents a theme followed by variations; and the finale transforms earlier material into a vigorous conclusion. The soloist must move between singing lines, sharply accented rhythms, and intricate passagework. It is one of the great twentieth-century concertos, demanding both technical range and an instinctive feel for Bartók's rhythmic language.

Listen for: the way folk-like gestures are transformed into large-scale symphonic argument.

Notable interpreters: Zoltán Székely (the dedicatee and first performer), Yehudi Menuhin, and Patricia Kopatchinskaja.

Editor's highlight

✦

One concerto
to seek out

A less-traveled masterpiece

Shostakovich
Violin Concerto No. 2

Shostakovich's Second Concerto deserves to be heard more often. It is not as immediately gripping as the First, nor does it offer the same monumental emotional arc. Instead, it rewards repeated listening: its restraint, dark humor, and abrupt shifts in character reveal a composer working with extraordinary economy and psychological subtlety.

Oistrakh premiered the work in September 1967 with the Moscow Philharmonic under Kirill Kondrashin. The concerto was written for Oistrakh's sixtieth birthday, but its mood is far from celebratory. It feels reflective, at times almost private, and its final pages leave a deliberately ambiguous impression.

Why it belongs here: it shows that virtuosity need not announce itself through constant brilliance. The soloist's task is to make a complicated emotional world feel inevitable.

Listening tip

Try the same work twice.

First, listen without following the score or reading a program note. Then return to the work and notice how the soloist shapes transitions, how the orchestra responds, and where the music changes direction. A concerto often becomes more rewarding when its large-scale design begins to emerge.
